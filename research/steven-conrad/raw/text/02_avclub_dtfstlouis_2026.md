# Title: Interview: Creator Steven Conrad on DTF St. Louis

## Q: What made you pivot from adapting a New Yorker article to working on something original?

SC: I'd never done a series with a relationship to a real story like that article. I recognized the possibility of its setting with middle-aged people still hungry in their sexual lives. I wanted to write about that. The challenge was fitting my make-believe world into real circumstances, but I didn't want to misattribute qualities to real people. David Harbour and I focused on "middle-aged emptiness and a bad decision that involved sex" instead.

The watershed moment was the Ashley Madison-style hookup app idea. People diving into that promise of consequence-free affairs seemed unwise. I wondered why smart people would do this. The answer becomes desperation to feel something. Once we thought of the DTF St. Louis app, we imagined a detective discovering seemingly normal people with secret passions. A friend told me about being "married to his spouse but also married to his kinks," saying he has obligations to them since they're permanent. That influenced how we developed the show's central tension.

## Q: How did you know David was the right fit for Floyd Smernitch? How did casting Jason Bateman and Linda Cardellini affect character development?

SC: David was already on the project. I recognize him as having tremendous empathy. He's a "march-forward performer" who keeps trying to save himself despite bad circumstances—like Floyd. David has this quality Gene Wilder possessed: you couldn't take your eyes off him, despite seeming like an unlikely leading man. He also brings sexuality that creates real power.

Jason's intelligence as an actor lets audiences believe he sees something beautiful in Floyd. Linda has the capacity to embody Carol's complexity—struggling financially and domestically, yet possessing untapped sexual dynamics. She can make audiences believe both sides exist in one person.

## Q: Can you discuss the non-linear structure and repeated scenes with additional context?

SC: The variations show shared events between three intimate characters, one of whom dies. Detective Donoghue Homer, played by Richard Jenkins, struggles understanding this freedom of sexuality. His imagination is opened by plausibility, while his younger partner Jodie emphasizes that people are more complicated than expected.

Episodes five, six, and seven journey toward truth. The big question is whether the detective accepts what he discovers about what's actually happening.

## On Character and Secrets (from The Wrap interview)

"In this show, Clark meets a person new in his life who excites him and who says to him, 'You're safe with me. You have these kinks. You can tell me what they are, and I'm not going to shame you.'"

"One of the frustrations of Clark's life is that he's been suppressing these different ideas of sexuality, so he gives these secrets to Carol. What does she then have? She has somebody's secrets, and they are treasures."

"Who's gaining what under what terms? That's a question I'm very much hoping to provoke."
