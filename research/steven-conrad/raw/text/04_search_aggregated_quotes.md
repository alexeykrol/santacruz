## On Writing Volume & Craft

"You have to write in multiples of three and four if you ever want to see another film produced. You grind out four screenplays, make them the very best they can be... and then the rest of it's never up to you."

"For any craft, practice is the key. Even if you have a good idea, practice at the craft of telling it."

"I think it's essential to fill that garbage can and get rid of that young writing."

First-time writers often produce work that is "very confessional or generally about yourself," which "makes for the least interesting story" — while you can write about yourself, focusing only on your specific experiences causes you to forget you're sharing with an audience.

---

## On Finding Story

"Writers need to discover a unique setting in a very strict circumstance that hasn't been mined to death in films before."

For Unfinished Business: idea came from observing businessmen on planes during the Walter Mitty production — watching them spend hours on spreadsheets with total dedication to their work.

"Know what you're trying to accomplish. You can't start a movie much less finish one if you don't know what you want to accomplish."

---

## On Preparation: Movie Models

Sometimes in preparing to write a particular script, Conrad starts with "movie models" — making a list of twenty movies to see what a movie felt like. "If I don't have one, it's kind of hard for me to write."

---

## On Writer's Block

"I'd find a movie that I've never seen, then I'd loop the movie on my TV and turn the volume off. I'll just look and see these images connecting, not knowing how they connect. Then I start writing my own story connecting what I think is happening."

---

## On Process: Long-form vs Film

"Premium TV is a writer's medium now. I wanted to dig into long-form storytelling."

There's always some "unfinished business" — it's never a question of burning out or fading away, but of simply keeping on.

---

## On Narrative Structure (Patriot)

"Nothing in 'Patriot' is meant to be extraneous. The failure of Birdbath to account for the difference in weight between American and European coins directly kicks off the season's most significant plot event. All human vulnerabilities in the world around John add up and integrate into making completion of John's task more difficult."

Conrad had "the first two seasons in his mind the entire time." (from composer Alex Wurman)

---

## On Dialogue Rhythm (Perpetual Grace)

"Rhythm, generally speaking, and some odd punctuation." When characters speak, it's "like they're trading solos" where "they have distinct delivery systems, but they hit the rhythm identically."

---

## On Endings

Conrad learned early in his career not to write past your ending and to know your ending before you start — a principle he applies to each episode, season, and the entire series.

---

## On Character Depth

Conrad believes there are no one-dimensional characters. When writing 10+ hours of television, even characters who might be the "evil guy" need depth — audiences must see "that person's humanity, their frailty, and the people that they love."

---

## On Mood

Conrad and his collaborators discuss mood constantly, emphasizing that every cinematic decision "has to create a mood."

"When your show needs to become more complex, then maybe it's time for your characters to become more complex."

---

## On DTF St. Louis / Human Desperation

Main theme: "20 years of carrying a rock up a hill is a lot." He noticed friends "doing impulsive, dangerous things to satisfy some desire or some hole in their lives."

Used Alfred Hitchcock as a model, often pondering what the great masters of thrills and suspense would do if they were telling a story as salacious as this one.

---

## On Patriot's Tone

Conrad used "bait-and-switch" — "they were banking that the spy genre was going to do a little more legwork than the show that we all privately had in mind."

The feature landscape became "primarily IP and superhero movies" while TV had "depth then, and an ambition, on the heels of very successful long-term shows like Sopranos and Mad Men."

---

## On Influences (Creative DNA)

- George Carlin — comedy that challenges sacred cows
- Lucille Ball — beauty through comedy, not appearance
- Charlie Chaplin — films before genre conventions existed
- George Saunders — compassion for overlooked characters
- Richard Ford's Independence Day — immersive literary reading
